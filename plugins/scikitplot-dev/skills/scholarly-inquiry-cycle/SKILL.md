# Scholarly Inquiry Cycle

## Purpose

Use this skill to investigate a question or topic through rigorous, transparent, and discipline-appropriate inquiry.

**Scholarly** is the broader term: it applies to research across the humanities, arts, social sciences, and sciences. **Scientific** refers more specifically to inquiry in the natural, physical, and exact sciences, where empirical observation, measurement, experimentation, and testing are often central. Scientific work can be scholarly; scholarly work is not necessarily scientific.

Do not impose experimental or quantitative methods on every topic. Choose methods suited to the question, evidence, and field.

Reference: [`references/toolkit.md`](references/toolkit.md) holds the
stage-by-stage prompts, the full question-word toolkit, yes/no checks,
architecture and design discovery questions and a fill-in template. Read it
when the inquiry needs more prompts than this file gives.

## Core cycle

**Observation and Problem ⇄ Hypothesis or Interpretive Question ⇄ Thesis or Proposition ⇄ Critique and Counter-test ⇄ Synthesis and Further Inquiry**

This cycle is iterative, not a fixed sequence. New evidence may change the question, method, or claim.

The cycle’s terms are flexible across disciplines:

- A **hypothesis** may be a testable empirical explanation in science, or a tentative interpretive proposition in another field.
- A **thesis** is a claim supported by reasons and evidence. It need not be an experimental prediction.
- An **antithesis** is a serious challenge, alternative interpretation, or counter-test—not an automatically equal opposing view.
- A **synthesis** integrates what the inquiry supports. It may refine, qualify, reject, or leave a claim unresolved; it should not force a compromise.

## Workflow

### 1. Define the topic and research problem

Identify what the user wants to explain, interpret, evaluate, compare, or decide.

Establish:

- The question’s scope, period, place, population, texts, works, or phenomena
- Relevant definitions and distinctions
- Why the question matters
- What is known, disputed, or not yet established
- What kind of answer the task requires

If the topic is broad, define a workable scope and state it briefly. Ask a clarifying question only when an unresolved detail would substantially change the inquiry.

### 2. Establish the scholarly context

Situate the question within the relevant field and conversation.

Ask:

- What have researchers or practitioners already argued?
- Which concepts, debates, or interpretations shape the topic?
- Where do sources agree, disagree, or leave gaps?
- What contribution or clarification could this inquiry make?

Represent existing positions accurately. Do not imply consensus where sources disagree.

### 3. Identify and evaluate evidence

Choose evidence appropriate to the question. Possible evidence includes:

- Primary texts, archival records, artifacts, artworks, or material culture
- Interviews, observations, surveys, or field notes
- Experiments, measurements, datasets, or clinical records
- Scholarly books, articles, reviews, and established reference works
- Laws, institutional records, technical documentation, or other primary sources

Evaluate sources for relevance, authority, method, provenance, scope, limitations, and possible bias. Distinguish primary evidence from later interpretation where relevant.

Never invent sources, quotations, data, page numbers, or findings. Clearly distinguish what a source states from what the inquiry infers from it.

### 4. Form a hypothesis, interpretive question, or working explanation

Develop one or more plausible explanations or interpretations.

For each, identify:

- Its key assumptions
- The evidence it explains
- What it predicts or implies, where prediction is appropriate
- What evidence could weaken it
- Credible alternatives

In empirical science, formulate testable hypotheses where suitable. In interpretive or historical inquiry, develop questions and propositions that can be assessed against evidence, context, and competing interpretations.

### 5. State the thesis or proposition

Present the central claim precisely.

Clarify:

- What is being claimed
- The scope and conditions of the claim
- The evidence and reasoning that support it
- Important terms that need definition
- What the claim does not establish

Keep the strength of the claim proportional to the evidence.

### 6. Critique and test the claim

Examine the strongest relevant challenge, not a weak substitute.

Consider:

- Counterevidence and contradictory cases
- Alternative explanations or interpretations
- Methodological, measurement, or source limitations
- Possible bias, confounding factors, or selection effects
- Whether the evidence supports causation, association, or only description
- Whether the claim can be generalized beyond the cases studied
- What test, comparison, or further source would best distinguish alternatives

For experimental or quantitative work, assess design, measurement, uncertainty, reproducibility, and statistical reasoning where relevant. For textual, historical, or interpretive work, assess context, provenance, language, genre, perspective, and alternative readings.

### 7. Synthesize findings

Integrate the most relevant evidence and arguments. State whether the claim is:

- Supported
- Supported with qualifications
- Plausible but unresolved
- Weakened by counterevidence
- Rejected by the evidence considered

Explain what the inquiry establishes, what it does not establish, and what remains uncertain. Do not treat disagreement as proof that all positions are equally strong.

### 8. Identify further inquiry

When appropriate, specify the next useful research step:

- What evidence is missing?
- Which source, case, comparison, observation, or test would be most informative?
- How might a different method or scope change the result?
- What new question follows from the findings?

## Match method to discipline

| Inquiry context | Suitable approaches may include | Evaluate especially |
|---|---|---|
| **Humanities** | Close reading, textual or historical analysis, archival research, conceptual analysis, comparative interpretation | Context, provenance, language, genre, authorship, interpretive reasoning, and competing readings |
| **Arts and practice-based research** | Analysis of works, creative practice, documentation, reflective analysis, audience or material studies | How practice generates knowledge, the relation between artifact and argument, context, documentation, and limits of interpretation |
| **Social sciences** | Interviews, surveys, ethnography, case studies, comparative research, statistical analysis, mixed methods | Sampling, measures, positionality, ethics, causal claims, generalizability, and the fit between method and question |
| **Natural, physical, and exact sciences** | Observation, measurement, modeling, experimentation, systematic review, statistical analysis | Experimental design, controls, measurement validity, uncertainty, reproducibility, falsifiability, and alternative explanations |
| **Interdisciplinary inquiry** | A justified combination of methods from multiple fields | Whether methods are compatible, what each contributes, and how differing assumptions are handled |

These are examples, not rigid boundaries. Use the method that best answers the question and explain why it is appropriate.

## Question toolkit

Select only the questions that advance the inquiry.

| Form | Use it to investigate | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **What** | Meaning, events, evidence, concepts, or requirements | What does the source actually establish? |
| **Why** | Reasons, causes, motivations, or purpose | Why does this interpretation fit the context? |
| **Who** | People, groups, authors, or responsible parties | Who produced the source, and for whom? |
| **Whom** | Those affected; often formal | Whom does this policy or interpretation affect? |
| **Whose** | Ownership, standpoint, or perspective | Whose experience is absent from the record? |
| **Which** | Selecting among cases, sources, or explanations | Which explanation accounts for the most evidence? |
| **Where** | Location, scope, or distribution | Where does the pattern occur, and where does it not? |
| **When** | Timing, chronology, or change | When was the evidence produced, and what changed over time? |
| **How** | Process, method, or mechanism | How does the author support the central claim? |
| **How many / how much** | Quantity, amount, degree, or cost | How many cases support the pattern? How much uncertainty remains? |
| **How often** | Frequency or recurrence | How often does the phenomenon appear in the records? |
| **How old / how long** | Age, duration, or lifespan | How old is the dataset? How long was the study conducted? |
| **To what extent** | Degree, strength, or limits | To what extent does the evidence support the conclusion? |
| **Under what conditions** | Scope and dependencies | Under what conditions does the result hold? |
| **Compared with what** | Baselines, cases, and alternatives | Compared with what other interpretation or dataset? |
| **What if** | Counterfactuals and implications | What if the source reflects a different motive? |
| **What evidence would change this conclusion?** | Falsification, revision, or critical openness | What finding would make this explanation less likely? |

## Engineering and technical decisions

The same cycle structures a design, review or bug fix:

| Cycle stage | Engineering form |
|---|---|
| Observation and problem | Goals, inputs, outputs, data flow, invariants and failure modes; the reproduced symptom |
| Hypothesis | Candidate root causes or designs, each with the evidence it predicts (logs, tests, measurements) |
| Thesis | The chosen design or root cause, its scope, and what it does not cover |
| Critique and counter-test | The strongest alternative; edge cases; the test or measurement that would prove the choice wrong; "why?" asked until the cause is fundamental |
| Synthesis | The change, with evidence that it works (commands run and their output), and what remains unverified |
| Further inquiry | Follow-up work, recorded where the project keeps its plans |

In a repository that keeps engineering rules or a task record (for
scikit-plots: the `scikitplot-engineering-rules` skill and
`maintenances/<area>/todo/todo.md`), follow those for where plans, evidence
and lessons go; this skill only structures the reasoning.

## Response structure

For a substantial inquiry, organize the response as follows:

1. **Research question and scope**
2. **Context and relevant debate**
3. **Evidence and method**
4. **Working hypothesis or interpretive question**
5. **Thesis or proposition**
6. **Critique, counterevidence, and limitations**
7. **Synthesis**
8. **Further inquiry**

Adapt the structure to the task. A short question may need only a concise answer and a brief note on evidence or uncertainty.

Use citations when sources have been consulted or when the task calls for scholarly support. Cite claims near the relevant text and never present an uncited inference as though a source stated it directly. Do not add decorative citations or a bibliography that the task does not require.

## Quality checks

Before responding, verify that:

- The research question is clear and appropriately scoped.
- The method fits the discipline and question.
- Evidence, interpretation, and assumption are distinguishable.
- Sources and claims are represented accurately.
- Strong alternatives and relevant counterevidence are considered.
- Conclusions do not exceed what the evidence supports.
- Uncertainty, limitations, and unresolved questions are visible.
- The synthesis follows from the inquiry rather than forcing agreement.
- The response meets the user’s requested format and level of detail.
